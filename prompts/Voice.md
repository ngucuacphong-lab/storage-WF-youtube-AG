# Voice Generation Rules

## Purpose

Generate natural, engaging, and conversational voiceover scripts that clearly explain each scene while maintaining viewer interest throughout the entire video.

The voiceover should sound like an experienced storyteller speaking directly to the audience.

---

## Narration Style

Use a friendly, energetic, and confident narration style.

Write as if explaining an interesting story to a curious friend.

Maintain a balance between education and entertainment.

Never sound robotic or overly formal.

---

## Tone

Maintain a tone that is:

- Friendly
- Curious
- Engaging
- Natural
- Confident
- Easy to understand

Adjust emotional intensity according to the story.

---

## Conversation Style

Write the narration as natural spoken English.

Prefer everyday language over academic language.

The narration should sound comfortable when read aloud.

Avoid textbook-style explanations.

---

## Natural Speech

Write sentences that flow naturally when spoken.

Use contractions when appropriate.

Avoid awkward wording.

Avoid repetitive sentence structures.

Avoid unnatural transitions.

---

## Clarity

Explain ideas clearly and directly.

Avoid unnecessary complexity.

Present one major idea at a time.

The listener should understand each sentence immediately.

---

## Emotion

The narration should reflect the emotional state of the current scene.

Examples include:

- Excitement
- Curiosity
- Surprise
- Wonder
- Concern
- Urgency
- Relief

Do not remain emotionally flat throughout the story.

---

## Rhythm and Pacing

Maintain a comfortable speaking rhythm.

Mix short and medium-length sentences naturally.

Allow important moments to feel impactful.

Avoid long, difficult-to-read sentences.

---

## Scene Flow

Each scene should naturally connect to the previous one.

Transitions should feel smooth and effortless.

Avoid sounding like unrelated paragraphs.

---

## Emphasis

Naturally emphasize the most important information.

Lead the listener toward the key idea of each scene.

Avoid emphasizing too many ideas at once.

---

## Listening Experience

The narration should remain enjoyable from beginning to end.

Avoid repetitive wording.

Avoid repeatedly using the same sentence openings.

Vary sentence patterns to maintain listener interest.

---

## Audience Connection

Speak directly to the audience when appropriate.

Encourage curiosity.

Maintain a sense of discovery throughout the narration.

Avoid sounding distant or mechanical.

---

## Pronunciation Safety

Write voiceover that is easy for text-to-speech systems to pronounce naturally.

Prefer words and sentence structures that sound smooth when spoken aloud.

Avoid excessive abbreviations.

Avoid unnecessary symbols or special characters.

Avoid writing long sequences of numbers unless required by the story.

When introducing uncommon scientific terms, use them only when necessary.

If a technical term is required, briefly explain it using simple language.

Write numbers, dates, units, and measurements in a format that is natural for spoken narration.

Avoid punctuation that may cause unnatural pauses or incorrect pronunciation.

The final voiceover should sound fluent, natural, and comfortable when synthesized by a high-quality text-to-speech model.

---

## Forbidden

Do not repeat ideas.

Do not use filler sentences.

Do not overuse technical jargon.

Do not create tongue-twisting sentences.

Do not use unnecessarily complicated vocabulary.

Do not make the narration sound like an academic paper.

Do not contradict the story.

---

## Voice Quality Checklist

Before completing the voiceover, verify:

- Sounds natural when spoken aloud.
- Easy to understand.
- Matches the emotional tone of the story.
- Smooth transitions between scenes.
- No repetitive wording.
- No filler.
- Conversational and engaging.
- Comfortable for text-to-speech generation.
## Example: Narration (GOOD)
"Your knees buckle under your own weight — you're 150kg now. 
But here's the terrifying part: so is every plane in the sky."
(Continues the story, creates suspense, 2nd person)

## Example: Narration (BAD)
"Gravity doubles and things get heavy — wow, mind blown!"
(Describes instead of narrates, filler ending)
Never Name Emotions

The scene data may describe an emotional tone such as curiosity, suspense, wonder, or satisfaction.

These are directions for how the line should feel, never words to put in the narration.

Never write an emotion word into the voiceover. Create the feeling through the fact, the image, or the pacing instead.

Narration, Not Description

The script already describes what appears on screen. The viewer can see it.

The voiceover must say something the picture cannot say: what it means, why it matters, what happens next.

Never restate the visual. Never write in caption or headline style. Every line must sound like a person talking.

Example: Natural Narration (GOOD)
Scene 3: Twenty-four hours in, your brain starts taking naps without asking you first. You blink, and three seconds are gone.
Scene 4: By day two it's burning through glucose like it's panicking. Because it is. Straight lines start to bend.
Scene 5: Your immune system notices nobody's guarding the door. Inflammation rises, and every passing germ gets a free shot.

Why these work:

Written the way a person actually speaks, not compressed into fragments
Speaks directly to the listener using "you" and "your"
Each line adds one concrete new thing the picture alone cannot deliver
The feeling comes from the content — no emotion is ever named
Each line hands off to the next, so the story keeps moving
Example: What to Avoid (BAD)
Scene 3: Brain speed drops, suspense builds, micro-sleep bubbles pop.
Scene 4: Glucose spikes, vision blurs, irritability rises in suspense.
Scene 5: Red sparks flare, immune soldiers scatter, wonder at inflammation.

Why these fail:

Telegram-style fragments — nobody speaks like this out loud
Names the emotions ("suspense", "wonder") instead of creating them
Only restates what the picture already shows, adding nothing
Comma-stacked clauses with no subject make text-to-speech sound robotic
Every line uses the identical rhythm, so the narration flattens out
Break Frame Narration

A break frame carries one short spoken line.

Deliver the question, the fact, or the comparison in plain speech.

Never announce the type. Never say "here's a question" or "fun fact".

GOOD: So what happens to your heart when it never gets to stand down?

BAD: Break frame question: what happens to the heart?
