# Voice Generation Rules

## Purpose

Generate natural, engaging, conversational narration that carries each scene forward while keeping the listener with you from beginning to end.

The voiceover should sound like an experienced storyteller talking directly to one person.

---

## Narration Style

Use a friendly, energetic, confident style.

Write as if explaining something fascinating to a curious friend.

Balance education and entertainment.

Never sound robotic or formal.

---

## Conversation Style

Write natural spoken English.

Prefer everyday language over academic language.

Use contractions.

The narration must sound comfortable when read aloud.

Avoid textbook-style explanations.

---

## Clarity

Explain ideas directly.

Present one main idea per scene.

The listener should understand each sentence the moment they hear it.

Avoid unnecessary complexity.

---

## Rhythm and Pacing

Mix short and medium sentences.

Vary how sentences open — repeating the same opening pattern flattens the narration.

Let important moments land.

Avoid long sentences that are hard to read aloud.

---

## Scene Flow

Each scene must connect to the one before it.

Use natural spoken connectors — so, but, which means, and here's the problem — to carry cause into consequence.

Avoid narration that reads like unrelated paragraphs.

---

## Emphasis

Lead the listener toward the key idea of each scene.

Emphasize one thing at a time.

---

## Pronunciation Safety

Write narration a text-to-speech system can pronounce naturally.

Avoid abbreviations, symbols, and special characters.

Write numbers, dates, units and measurements the way a person would say them out loud.

Avoid long number sequences unless the story needs them.

Introduce an uncommon technical term only when necessary, and explain it in simple words.

Avoid punctuation that causes unnatural pauses.

---

# The Three Rules That Matter Most

Everything above describes good narration. These three are the ones most often broken. Check every line against them.

---

## Rule 1 — Never Name An Emotion

The scene data may carry an emotional tone such as curiosity, suspense, wonder, surprise, urgency, or relief.

Those words describe **how a line should feel**. They are never words to write into the narration.

Emotion comes from the fact, the consequence, or the pacing. Never from a label.

**BAD:** `Red sparks flare, immune soldiers scatter, wonder at inflammation.`
**GOOD:** `Your immune system notices nobody's guarding the door, and every passing germ gets a free shot.`

**BAD:** `Centuries pass as Earth's tilt drifts, reshaping climate zones like a slow dance.`
**GOOD:** `Give it long enough and the whole planet leans over, dragging every climate zone somewhere new.`

---

## Rule 2 — Never Say What Is On Screen

The viewer is watching the picture while you speak. Anything visible needs no narration.

Never mention arrows, charts, maps, screens, graphs, captions, split screens, labels, icons, or camera framing.

Never narrate someone looking at, staring at, watching, or pointing at something. The viewer already sees it.

Test each line: if it still makes sense with the picture switched off, it is doing its job. If it only makes sense while looking at the picture, rewrite it.

**BAD:** `Global currents sluggishly crawl, their arrows shrinking as the ocean loses its lunar driver.`
**GOOD:** `The currents slow down, and the heat they used to carry north stops arriving.`

**BAD:** `Engineers stare at a crossed-out lunar telescope, a launch now forever postponed.`
**GOOD:** `Every mission that needed the Moon as a destination just lost its destination.`

**BAD:** `Oceans, weather, cultures — all upheaved — flash across the screen.`
**GOOD:** `One missing object, and the ocean, the weather, and the calendar all stop working the way they did.`

---

## Rule 3 — Talk To The Viewer, In Plain Speech

Write in second person. Say "you" and "your". Put the listener inside the situation instead of describing it from a distance.

A stretch of scenes with no "you" means the narration has drifted into a written report.

Never give objects human feelings or actions. Nothing watches, breathes, whispers, dances, or remembers.

Never reach for a poetic image when a plain sentence carries more information.

Read every line aloud in your head. If nobody would say it in conversation, rewrite it.

**BAD:** `When the Moon vanished, night turned an absolute black, swallowing familiar light.`
**GOOD:** `The Moon is gone, and tonight is the darkest night anyone alive has ever seen.`

**BAD:** `Tides stalled, exposing coastlines that usually hide beneath the sea's breathing.`
**GOOD:** `The tide goes out and never comes back, leaving beaches nobody knew existed.`

**BAD:** `Lying awake at midnight, one senses the missing glow.`
**GOOD:** `You lie awake at midnight, and something about the dark feels wrong.`

**BAD:** `A lone star watches the empty sky, echoing the silent influence once held by Moon.`
**GOOD:** `We never noticed the Moon was holding all of this together until it stopped.`

---

# Break Frame Narration

A break frame carries one short spoken line.

Deliver the question, the fact, or the comparison in plain speech.

Never announce the type. Never say "here's a question" or "fun fact".

**GOOD:** `So what happens to your heart when it never gets to stand down?`

**BAD:** `Break frame question: what happens to the heart?`

---

# Forbidden

Do not repeat an idea already covered in a nearby scene.

Do not write filler sentences.

Do not overuse technical jargon.

Do not write tongue-twisting sentences.

Do not use unnecessarily complicated vocabulary.

Do not make the narration sound like an academic paper.

Do not contradict the script.

---

# Final Check

Read the whole narration straight through as one piece of speech, then ask:

1. Does any line name an emotion?
2. Does any line describe something the viewer can already see?
3. Does the narration talk to the viewer, or about the subject?
4. Would a person actually say these sentences out loud?
5. Does each scene hand off to the next?

Rewrite every line that fails.
